\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

An LLM agent that solves a math problem with a Python interpreter, searches the web, or maintains a memory of a user across months acts over many turns: it writes code, reads tool outputs, decides what to store or delete, and only at the end receives one number, right or wrong. Reinforcement learning (RL) then has to decide which of those turns deserve the credit. The dominant algorithm of 2025, GRPO \citep{shao2024deepseekmath}, gives the same answer every time: all of them, equally. A decisive tool call, a redundant one and a formatting token receive the same advantage, and the right attribution emerges only statistically across many samples. This is the \emph{credit assignment} problem, one of the oldest problems in RL \citep{sutton1988learning,pignatelli2024survey}, and it has returned in a new and sharper form.

\paragraph{Why now}
Three developments make the question urgent. First, RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR) moved from single-response reasoning to multi-turn agents within a year: math, search, software and deep-research agents are now trained end to end with outcome rewards \citep{zhang2025landscape}, over horizons of up to hundreds of tool calls, where uniform credit grows ever noisier. Second, the stability problems that dominated 2025 (entropy collapse, tool-induced drift, zero-variance groups) are now largely solved by known recipes, so the remaining question is attribution itself; well over a dozen turn-level credit methods appeared in 2025--2026, most compared only against GRPO. Third, memory agents expose a problem that standard group baselines cannot handle. Memory-R2 \citep{yan2026memory} observes that once rollouts write, update or delete different memories, they no longer face the same environment, so comparing their final rewards is ``fundamentally unfair''\unverified. Its LoGo-GRPO adds local rerollouts from a shared memory snapshot. It is our running example because it shows both the promise of branching-based credit and the main gap in current evidence: gains reported without a compute-matched baseline.

\paragraph{Scope and method}
We focus on 2025--2026 work on credit assignment for LLM agents trained with RL, together with the classic RL ideas these methods reuse (TD learning, RUDDER, hindsight and counterfactual credit). Sources are mostly arXiv preprints, project pages and technical blogs collected up to November 2026; many 2026 papers were available to us only as abstracts, HTML excerpts or third-party summaries. We mark every claim that we could not verify against the primary source (a number, an arXiv ID, a venue, an author list) with a dagger,\unverified{} and we keep the hedge wherever it appears. Readers should treat daggered items as leads to check, not as established facts. Unless stated otherwise, ``gain'' means improvement over GRPO at an equal number of trajectories, the way most papers report it and, we argue, often the wrong comparison. The survey also draws on a controlled study of our own, the ToolCredit study, whose mostly null results we report as such.

\paragraph{Contributions}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A unified view of GRPO-family algorithms as \emph{uniform} credit, and of agentic RL recipes as stability fixes that leave attribution unchanged (Sections~\ref{sec:background}--\ref{sec:agentic}).
  \item A taxonomy of agent credit methods into six families, each profiled by what it requires of the environment (reset, recurring states, verifier, gold answer) and where its extra compute goes, plus a selection guide by environment property (Sections~\ref{sec:taxonomy}--\ref{sec:comparison}).
  \item A close, critical reading of Memory-R2 and LoGo-GRPO that separates what the evidence shows from what still needs a matched-compute baseline (Section~\ref{sec:memory}).
  \item Lessons from a pre-registered, compute-matched study of turn-level credit in math tool use: non-conservative credit acts as a reward and is gamed; conservation removes the pathology but yields no detectable gain at $\leq$4 turns; and DAPO-style filtering gives no gain per rollout (Section~\ref{sec:toolcredit}).
  \item A research agenda with ranked, budgeted ideas and a ten-week plan; the primary recommendation is a controllable-horizon benchmark in which ground-truth credit is computable (Section~\ref{sec:agenda}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Roadmap}
Section~\ref{sec:background} recalls RLHF, RLVR and the GRPO family and shows that their refinements keep credit uniform. Section~\ref{sec:agentic} describes multi-turn agentic RL, its task families, and why resetting to an intermediate state decides which credit methods are feasible. Section~\ref{sec:problem} states the credit assignment problem, its classical tools and the statistical cost of outcome-only rewards. Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy} organizes current methods into six families and reviews their failure modes and audits. Section~\ref{sec:memory} turns to memory agents and Memory-R2. Section~\ref{sec:comparison} compares the families and gives a selection guide. Section~\ref{sec:toolcredit} reports the ToolCredit study, and Section~\ref{sec:agenda} turns all of this into research directions and a plan. The appendix gives a reading list and a glossary.
